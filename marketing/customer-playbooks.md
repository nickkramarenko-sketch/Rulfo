What to know before you call each type of long-term customer: how their freight moves, what they worry about, words they use, what to ask, what to offer. Built up one customer type at a time. Order follows the long-term ranking in `ideal-customer-profile.md`.

## 1. Importers / brands with steady containers

### How their freight moves
1. Factory (usually Asia) loads a 20' or 40' container. **Floor-loaded** = loose cartons stacked to the ceiling. **Palletized** = already on pallets.
2. Ocean carrier sails it to Oakland or LA/Long Beach (2–5 weeks).
3. Before it sails, their customs broker files the **ISF** (Importer Security Filing). On arrival the broker files **customs entry** and pays duties.
4. Container is unloaded from the ship to the terminal. The clock starts.
5. A **drayage** trucker picks it up on a **chassis** (the trailer frame under the container) and takes it to a warehouse.
6. Warehouse **devans / strips** it (unloads), counts, palletizes, wraps, labels.
7. Goods are stored, or reloaded into 53' trailers (**transload**) and sent to customers, Amazon, or their own DCs.
8. Empty container goes back to the terminal or a depot.

### The three clocks they fear (the reason they pay you)
| Fee | What it is | Who bills it |
|---|---|---|
| **Demurrage** | Container sits at the terminal past its free days | Ocean carrier / terminal |
| **Detention** | They have the container outside the terminal past its free days (not returned empty) | Ocean carrier |
| **Chassis per diem** | Daily rent on the chassis | Chassis pool, separate bill |

- Free time is set by the carrier's tariff or their service contract. It varies by carrier, so ask them what theirs is.
- Oakland cut free time from 7 to 4 days back in 2022 during congestion. Today's numbers depend on carrier and terminal, so don't quote them.
- **Last Free Day (LFD)** is the date that matters. Miss it and fees stack up daily.
- Oakland terminals also charge gate fees per container (one terminal: $45 from Nov 2025).

**Your pitch in one line:** "We empty your container fast so you stop paying detention and chassis rent, then store or ship the freight from Reno."

### Why ship to Reno instead of unloading near the port?
- Bay Area warehouse space is ~40% more expensive than Reno ($1.19–1.30 vs $0.80 per SF per month).
- Nevada has no inventory tax.
- Their customers are in Nevada, Utah, Idaho, or inland, so Reno is closer to the final buyer.
- Downside you must answer: the container travels 4 hours over Donner, so the drayage costs more and the container is out longer (detention). Solution: a fast turn (empty in 2 hours, back on the road), or transload near the port and send 53' trailers (holds ~30% more than a 40' container) to Reno.

### What they judge a warehouse on
1. **Total cost, not storage rate.** A cheap warehouse that misses the LFD costs more.
2. Speed: container emptied same day, empty returned fast.
3. Accuracy: counts match the packing list; damage photographed at unload.
4. Visibility: they want to see inventory and receipts online (your customer portal).
5. Clear pricing: per container, per pallet, storage per month, labor, extras.
6. Surge capacity at peak (tariff rushes, Q3–Q4).
7. References from other importers.

### KPIs to promise and track
- Container unload time (dock to empty)
- Dock-to-stock time (unloaded to put away and visible in the portal)
- Inventory accuracy (target 99.5%+)
- On-time ship rate
- Damage rate / claims
- Detention days caused by us (target: zero)

### Words they use
FCL / LCL, 20' / 40' / 40'HC, floor-loaded, palletized, devan / strip, transload, ISF, entry, LFD, demurrage, detention, per diem, chassis, empty return, packing list, ASN, SKU, carton count, OS&D (over, short and damaged).

### Questions to ask on the first call
1. How many containers a month, and through which port?
2. Floor-loaded or palletized? How many SKUs per container?
3. Where do the goods go after: your customers, Amazon, your own DC?
4. Who does your drayage now? Who's your customs broker?
5. How many free days do you get? Have you paid detention or demurrage this year?
6. Do you need storage, or straight transload?
7. Any labels, retail compliance, or Amazon prep?

### What to offer them (bundle = they stay)
Unload + palletize + photo receipt → store by the pallet → ship pallets or cases to their customers, Amazon or retailers → monthly report. Add-ons: re-label, kitting, inspection, returns.

### Red flags
- Goods need cold storage or food-grade certification
- Lithium batteries in bulk
- Wants floor-loaded unload at palletized prices
- No customs broker, or entries that keep getting held

## 2. California manufacturers + distributors needing a Nevada DC

### How they work
- They make or import goods in California and ship to stores, distributors and online buyers across the West.
- Today they store in their own building or a California 3PL. Pain starts when the building is full, the lease is up, or costs jump.
- A Nevada DC usually starts as **overflow** (slow movers and safety stock) and grows into their **main western DC** if it works.

### Why they move to Reno (their math)
- Rent: Bay Area $1.19–1.30/SF vs Reno ~$0.80/SF per month.
- Nevada: no inventory tax, no corporate or personal income tax.
- Industry sources claim power ~40% cheaper and labor ~30% cheaper than California (vendor numbers, use carefully).
- California rules add cost and paperwork for warehouses (example: AB 701 quota-disclosure law since 2022).
- One truck day from Reno reaches the Bay Area, Sacramento, LA, Portland, Boise, Salt Lake and Phoenix.
- Many keep their California office and production; only the inventory moves.

### What they judge a warehouse on
1. Inventory accuracy and a weekly or live inventory report (portal).
2. Order cut-off times: orders in by X ship same day.
3. Damage rate and claims handling.
4. Pallet and case picking for B2B orders (not piece-picking).
5. Carriers: who books the trucks, LTL rates, pickups.
6. Insurance certificate and clear liability limits.
7. Contract terms: 12–36 months, monthly minimum, rate increase cap.

### How they buy
- Decision is a team: operations or supply chain manager finds you, CFO/owner signs.
- Process: phone call → site visit → written proposal → 30–90 day trial or first SKUs → contract.
- Sales cycle: 1–3 months. Peak buying time: Q1 (budgets) and before Q3–Q4 peak.

### Questions to ask
1. How many pallets on average, and at peak?
2. How many SKUs? Inbound trucks per week? Outbound orders per day?
3. Who are your customers (retailers, distributors, online)?
4. What's not working with your current warehouse?
5. Do you need EDI or a system connection?
6. When does your lease or contract end?

### What to offer
Monthly storage per pallet + receiving + pick/ship per order or case + monthly minimum. Add-ons: labels, kitting, retail compliance, local delivery, returns.

### Red flags
Cold/frozen, food needing AIB certification, under 50 pallets, piece-pick e-commerce as the main job.

## 3. Brands shipping to big retailers (Walmart, Costco, Target, Home Depot)

### How they work
- The retailer sends a **purchase order (PO)**. The brand must deliver the exact quantity, on the exact day, labeled exactly the retailer's way.
- Every retailer has a **routing guide**: label format, pallet height, pallet type, appointment rules, paperwork.
- Two freight setups:
  - **Prepaid:** the brand books the truck and delivers to the retailer's DC.
  - **Collect:** the retailer's own carrier picks up from your dock on a scheduled day.
- Paperwork: **EDI 850** (PO in), **EDI 856 ASN** (ship notice out), **GS1-128 / SSCC** pallet and case labels, BOL.

### What they fear: chargebacks
- Retailers fine the brand for late, early, short or wrongly labeled deliveries.
- Walmart **OTIF** (on-time in-full), per a 2026 vendor guide: 90% on-time for prepaid, 98% collect-ready, 95% in-full; fine ~3% of the cost of goods on non-compliant cases. Confirm in Walmart's supplier docs.
- Every retailer has its own version. Fines can wipe out the brand's margin.

**Your pitch:** "We build, label and ship your retail orders to the routing guide, book the appointments, and send you proof. Fewer chargebacks."

### What they judge a warehouse on
1. Chargeback history: do you know the routing guides?
2. Appointment booking and on-time pickups/deliveries.
3. Pallet build quality (height, overhang, wrap, labels on 2 sides).
4. ASN accuracy (what you ship matches what the system says).
5. Fast turnaround: PO in Monday, ship Wednesday.

### Questions to ask
1. Which retailers, how many POs a month?
2. Prepaid or collect?
3. Chargebacks last year? For what?
4. Do you send EDI yourself or need us to?
5. Pallet or case orders? Mixed pallets?

### What to offer
Storage + pallet build + retail labels + appointment booking + BOL + photo of every outbound pallet. Later: EDI connection.

### What you'll need
GS1 label printer, routing guide binder per retailer, appointment portal logins (each retailer uses its own), trained crew on pallet build rules.

## 4. Amazon / e-commerce brands with bulk inventory

### How they work
- Amazon limits how much stock a seller can keep in Amazon warehouses (**capacity limits**; cut sharply in 2025). The rest must sit somewhere else: a 3PL.
- The 3PL holds bulk pallets and sends **replenishment** shipments to Amazon FBA (and Walmart WFS) every week or two.
- Amazon stopped doing prep and labeling for sellers on Jan 1, 2026. So the 3PL labels (FNSKU), bags, bundles and puts the carton labels on.
- Inbound options: **Minimal shipment splits** (fewer Amazon warehouses, higher placement fee) vs **Amazon-optimized** (more splits, lower fee). Amazon's partnered carrier or AWD are other routes.
- 2026 changes (per seller-tool blogs, verify in Seller Central): FBA fees up Jan 15, 2026; AWD stopped accepting large units (18x14x8 in or 20 lb+) from July 31, 2026, so bulky sellers need a 3PL buffer; AWD no longer preps.

### What they fear
- Running out of stock on Amazon (ranking drops).
- Shipments rejected for bad prep or labels.
- Long-term storage fees at Amazon.

### What they judge a 3PL on
1. Knows Amazon prep and label rules.
2. Fast turnaround on replenishment (24–48 hours).
3. Ship to Amazon by LTL / partnered carrier, book appointments.
4. Inventory visibility.
5. Price per unit for prep (they compare online).

### Questions to ask
1. How many SKUs, units per month to Amazon?
2. Where is your bulk stock now? Do you import containers?
3. What prep do your products need?
4. Also on Walmart, Shopify, retail?
5. Oversize products? (AWD change = new need)

### What to offer
Container unload → bulk storage by pallet → weekly Amazon/Walmart replenishment with prep → monthly report. Price prep per unit, storage per pallet.

### Fit note
Good when they store pallets with you. Avoid if it's only piece-prep with no storage.

## 5. Building products + industrial distributors

### How they work
- Distributors of tile, flooring, roofing, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, pipe, MRO and mining supplies buy by the truckload from manufacturers and sell in smaller amounts to contractors and branches.
- They need stock near the jobs. Reno growth (new subdivisions, data centers, mines) means more stock in Northern Nevada.
- Freight is heavy, often long or odd-sized, sometimes on flatbeds.

### What they fear
- Damage (tile breaks, pipe bends).
- Missing a job-site delivery date (crew standing around).
- Losing track of lots (tile color lots must match on one job).

### What they judge a warehouse on
1. Heavy-capacity forklifts and floor; space for long items.
2. Lot tracking.
3. Scheduled job-site and branch deliveries.
4. Careful handling, photo on receipt and ship.
5. Will-call (contractors picking up at your dock).

### Questions to ask
1. What products, how heavy, any long items?
2. How many trucks in a month? How many deliveries out?
3. Job sites, branches, or contractor pickups?
4. Do you need lot or color tracking?
5. Flatbed or dry van?

### What to offer
Storage + receiving + order pick + will-call + local delivery (partner carrier). Add-on: project staging for big jobs.

## 6. Freight forwarders + customs brokers

### How they work
- **Freight forwarder / NVOCC:** books ocean or air space for importers and arranges everything door to door. They sell the whole move and buy pieces from partners (drayage, warehouse, trucking).
- **Customs broker:** licensed to file customs entries and ISF for the importer. Often in the same company as the forwarder.
- They bill the importer and pay you. Their margin is the difference, so they want clear, stable rates.
- They have many importer clients. One good forwarder relationship = many containers a month.

### What they need from you
1. Fast quotes (same day, often within the hour).
2. Reliable updates without chasing: container in, unloaded, counts, damage, ready to ship.
3. Photos and documents they can forward to their client (POD, OS&D report).
4. Paying on time on their side; they pay Net 30–45 to you.
5. You don't go around them to their client. Never steal their customer, or you lose them all.

### How to win them
- Start with one container. Over-communicate on it.
- Send a short daily or per-container status email.
- Give them a rate sheet they can mark up.
- Visit or call the branch manager monthly.

### Questions to ask
1. How many containers a month go to Nevada / inland West / Utah / Idaho?
2. Who handles those now? What goes wrong?
3. Floor-loaded or palletized? Storage or straight transload?
4. Do your clients need Amazon prep or retail labels?

### Words they use
NVOCC, HBL / MBL (house / master bill of lading), ISF, entry, arrival notice, PODs, DO (delivery order), LFD, door move.

## 7. Freight brokers + LTL carriers

### How they work
- **Freight broker:** finds a truck for a shipper's load, takes a margin. Doesn't own trucks. Uses a TMS and load boards.
- **LTL carrier:** moves partial loads through its own terminals.
- Brokers use a cross-dock to: split a truckload into several deliveries, combine partial loads into one truck, hold freight when the receiver can't take it, or swap trailers.

### The 2026 fraud problem (important)
- Cargo theft and fraud are up sharply: CargoNet estimated ~$725M in 2025 losses (+60%); Q1 2026 fake-identity load thefts up 31%, nearly half in California.
- Thieves pose as real carriers or brokers, hack emails, and double-broker loads.
- **For you:** verify every pickup. Driver name, truck and trailer number, MC/DOT number, photo of driver license and truck, call the broker back on a number you already have (not the one in the email). Never release freight on an email change alone.
- **Selling point:** "We verify every driver and photograph every pickup." Brokers want warehouses that protect them.

### What brokers judge a cross-dock on
1. Answers the phone 24/7, quotes fast.
2. Turn time (truck in and out fast).
3. Accurate counts and photos.
4. Easy setup (W-9, insurance certificate, rate sheet, no contract).
5. Fair accessorials (no surprise fees).

### Payment
Net 30 typical; some brokers pay slow. Check broker credit (DAT or similar) and take card or quick-pay for the first 3 loads.

### Questions to ask
1. Which lanes through Reno?
2. How many loads a week?
3. Split, consolidate, overnight hold, or storage?
4. Who's your current dock in Reno, and what goes wrong?

## 8. Project freight (solar, data centers)

### How it works
- A developer hires a general contractor or EPC (engineering, procurement and construction company). They order equipment months early.
- Equipment arrives before the site can take it. It needs a secure place to wait, then gets delivered to the site in a planned order (**call-offs**).
- **Solar:** modules (~30 per pallet), inverters, racking steel, cable. Projects near Reno: Sierra Solar 400 MW (Churchill County, due 2027), Libra Solar 700 MW (Yerington, end 2027). Battery containers are hazmat and not pallets.
- **Data centers:** racks, servers, busway, cable, cooling, switchgear, transformers. TRIC: Switch, Apple, Tract/Fleet ($10B, 400 MW). Some items are oversize (crane + yard).

### What they fear
- Damage or missing parts holding up the build (crews cost thousands per hour).
- Theft (copper cable, modules).
- Wrong item sent to site.

### What they judge a warehouse on
1. Security: fenced, cameras, caged area, controlled access.
2. Serialized receiving: every crate and serial number logged with photos.
3. Insurance: often $5M+ liability and cargo cover; their vendor approval forms.
4. Scheduled site delivery (flatbeds, liftgates, exact times).
5. Daily inventory and status reports.

### How they buy
- Vendor approval first (insurance, safety program, W-9, references), then a master services agreement + a work order per project.
- 1–3 month sales cycle. Contracts run as long as the project (6–18 months).

### Questions to ask
1. Project name, location, start and end dates?
2. What equipment and how many pallets/crates? Any oversize?
3. Delivery schedule to site?
4. Insurance and security requirements?
5. Who approves vendors?

### Before you pitch
$5M liability, cargo insurance, cameras, caged area, written receiving SOP with photos, safety program.
